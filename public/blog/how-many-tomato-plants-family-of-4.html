How many tomato plants for a family of 4? The real numbers.

By Urban Root · Published 18 May 2026 · 7 min read

The answer runs from 7 plants to more than 60, and the difference is entirely about what you plan to do with them. Here is the actual math, on the yields and spacings the Homestead Plan calculator uses.

Quick answer for a family of 4, from the Self-Sufficiency calculator with slicing, cherry and paste tomatoes each set to Weekly:
Fresh eating only (salads, sandwiches, snacking): 18 plants (7 slicing, 6 cherry, 5 paste).
Fresh + some preserving (a few quarts of sauce, salsa): 26 plants (10 slicing, 8 cherry, 8 paste).
Full-year self-sufficiency (a sauce pantry, canned tomatoes, dried): 34 plants minimum (13 slicing, 11 cherry, 10 paste). A true 60-quart sauce year takes 60 paste plants on its own.

Where the numbers come from

USDA Economic Research Service tracks per-capita tomato availability at around 21 pounds of fresh-market tomatoes per person per year, plus another 70 pounds of processed tomato equivalent (sauce, paste, juice, ketchup). Home gardeners eat differently (far more fresh, often less processed), so the planning numbers we use are biased toward heavier fresh consumption and toward putting up your own sauce rather than buying it.

Our base assumption for a homestead garden: 25 pounds of slicing tomatoes per person per year, fresh-equivalent, before processing losses. For a family of four, that is 100 pounds total at the full-year goal. Tick cherry and paste tomatoes too and the calculator adds 8 and 12 pounds per person for them. The fresh-only goal plans half of each amount and fresh + some preserving three quarters. Weekly plans the full amount for a type, and Sometimes, the setting a newly ticked crop starts on, plans half. A family that loves tomatoes eats 35–40 pounds per person. A family that eats them mostly in summer salads is closer to 12–15 pounds per person.

Scenario 1: Fresh eating only

You want sliced tomato on sandwiches all summer, a tomato in the salad most nights, a tray of bruschetta when friends are over. You are not canning. You will not freeze sauce. Any surplus goes to neighbours.

Target: 50 lb of slicing tomatoes across 12–14 productive weeks (about July through mid-September in zone 6, longer in warmer zones), plus 16 lb of cherry tomatoes and 24 lb of paste tomatoes for fresh salsa and quick weeknight sauce.

  • 7 indeterminate slicing plants × 8 lb = 56 lb. Pick three or four varieties with different colours and maturity dates: a Black Krim, a Cherokee Purple, a yellow heirloom, a classic red.
  • 6 cherry plants × 3 lb = 18 lb. Cherry tomatoes are the snacking, salad, and pasta-on-a-weeknight crop. They produce until frost.
  • 5 paste plants × 5 lb = 25 lb, for salsa and sauce eaten the same week.

Total: 18 plants, 72 square feet of bed, 99 lb at the conservative yield against a 90 lb target.

Scenario 2: Fresh plus light preserving

You want all the fresh eating from scenario 1, plus 20–30 quarts of sauce or salsa in the freezer for winter. Maybe a few jars of canned diced tomatoes if the canner comes out.

Sauce reduction is the math that surprises people. A finished quart of thick tomato sauce takes about 5 pounds of fresh paste tomatoes (or 6–7 pounds of slicing tomatoes, which have more water). 25 quarts of sauce = 125 lb of paste tomatoes. That is most of your garden.

The trick is dedicating paste varieties (San Marzano, Amish Paste, Roma VF) to the sauce job. They have lower water content, thicker walls, and reduce faster.

  • 10 slicing plants × 8 lb = 80 lb fresh (everyday eating, gifts, salads).
  • 8 cherry plants × 3 lb = 24 lb (snacks, pasta, gifts).
  • 8 paste plants × 5 lb = 40 lb (about 8 quarts of sauce in the freezer).

That is the calculator's answer for this goal: 26 plants, 104 sq ft of bed. A true 25-quart pantry takes 25 paste plants on their own (125 lb at 5 lb per plant). Sauce yield is the bottleneck, not slicing yield.

Realistic combination for "scenario 2 with a real sauce supply": 43 plants, ~172 sq ft (10 slicing, 8 cherry, 25 paste, using the per-type spacing above).

Scenario 3: Full-year self-sufficiency

This is the homestead scenario. You want 60–100 quarts of finished sauce, plus canned whole tomatoes, plus dried tomato halves, plus all the fresh you can eat. You are putting tomatoes into the pantry like grocery insurance.

100 quarts of sauce × 5 lb / quart = 500 lb of paste tomatoes. At 5 lb per plant conservative yield, that is 100 paste plants. Most families settle for 60–80 quarts and 60–80 paste plants. Add slicing and cherry on top.

  • 13 slicing plants × 8 lb = 104 lb fresh (heavy fresh eating, gifts, food swap with neighbours).
  • 11 cherry plants × 3 lb = 33 lb (snacks, dehydrated, salad).
  • 10 paste plants × 5 lb = 50 lb → roughly 10 quarts of sauce.

For a realistic full year: 34 plants minimum, 136 sq ft of bed. That is the calculator's full-year answer, sized on what the family eats. To hit 60 quarts of sauce, scale paste to 60 plants: 84 plants in all. Many homesteaders find it easier to plant the full sauce target every other year, rotate the bed to a heavy feeder in between, and rely on canned reserves in the off year.

What we get wrong (and how to plan around it)

Variety mix matters more than total count

40 indeterminate slicers will not give you sauce. They will give you 320 lb of beautiful tomatoes that take twice as long to cook down because their water content is 95%. Match variety to job: paste varieties for sauce, slicing for fresh, cherry for snacks and dehydration.

Determinate vs indeterminate

Determinate tomatoes (Roma VF, Celebrity, Bush Early Girl) set fruit over a 2–3 week window and then stop. Excellent for canning days. Indeterminate tomatoes (most heirlooms, San Marzano) keep flowering and fruiting until frost. Excellent for trickle harvest into the kitchen. A mixed planting evens out the workload.

Succession planting buys time

If you have the space and the growing season, start a second batch of 4–6 paste tomatoes from seed 4 weeks after the first transplant date. They will catch the end of the season and add 25 lb of late-summer paste to the canning queue.

Failures happen

Early blight, late blight, hornworms, drought stress, a wet July. Plan with 10–15% buffer on plant counts if your region is prone to one of these. A 60-plant sauce target becomes a 70-plant plan.
